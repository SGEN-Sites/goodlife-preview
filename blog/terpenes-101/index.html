Terpenes: follow your nose

The names on our product pages, translated into smells you already know.

Ever notice how one jar smells like lemon peel and another like cracked pepper? That's terpenes. Many products on our New York menus list the terpenes measured for them, and once you know the names, those lists turn into a map of flavors you might like. Here's how to read them (for adults 21+, like everything on this site).

What terpenes are

Terpenes are aroma compounds. Cannabis makes them, and so do plenty of other plants — the same family of compounds is behind the smell of citrus peel, black pepper, pine needles and lavender. They're a big reason two products with the same strain type can smell completely different, and why a fresh jar can fill a room.

This post sticks to aroma. How any product feels is personal — everyone's experience is different — so use terpenes to find flavors you enjoy, not to predict how a product will hit.

Find them on the menu

Open a product page and, on many items, you'll see a Terpenes section: the top terpenes measured for that product, listed from most to least, sometimes with a total. They show up most on flower, pre-rolls and vapes, and on some edibles, drinks, tinctures and concentrates too. Some brands report them in mg/g, others as a percentage — 10 mg/g is the same as 1%.

Those Greek letters are just chemistry. The α (alpha) and β (beta) in front of a name mark closely related versions of the same compound, like α-pinene and β-pinene.

When we wrote this, β-caryophyllene was the terpene listed most often on our New York menus — and most often at the top of the list — with limonene, α-humulene, linalool and β-myrcene close behind.

The big seven, by smell

TerpeneAromaAlso found in
β-MyrceneEarthy, musky, a little clove-likeHops, lemongrass, thyme
LimoneneStrong, bright citrusLemon and orange peel
β-CaryophylleneSpicy, peppery, woodyBlack pepper, cloves
Pinene (α and β)Fresh pine, herbaceousPine needles, rosemary
LinaloolFloral and sweet, a hint of spiceLavender
α-HumuleneEarthy, woody, hoppyHops, which it is named after
TerpinoleneFresh, piney, floral, herbalTea tree, nutmeg

You'll spot others on our product pages, too: α-bisabolol (soft and floral, a big part of chamomile's scent), guaiol (woody), geraniol (rosy, like geraniums) and eucalyptol (cool and fresh, as in eucalyptus).

Reading a terpene list

Start with the top two or three names, since they're the most abundant. As a rough guide, a list led by β-caryophyllene and limonene points to pepper and citrus, while one led by terpinolene points fresher and more floral. The total tells you how much was measured overall. It's useful for comparing, but a bigger number isn't automatically a better product.

Train your nose

Want a head start? Raid the spice rack. Crack some black pepper, sniff a clove, rub a rosemary sprig, zest a lemon, open the lavender. Those are your reference points for β-caryophyllene, pinene, limonene and linalool. Once they click, a terpene list starts to read like a flavor description. And keep your jars sealed — good storage keeps that aroma where it belongs.

Shop by nose

  1. 01

    Step 1: Start with a favorite

    Pull up something you've enjoyed and note the first two or three terpenes on its page.

  2. 02

    Step 2: Compare

    Open a few similar products and look for the same names near the top of the list.

  3. 03

    Step 3: Ask at the counter

    Bring your list in. The Good Life team is here to guide you.

  4. 04

    Step 4: Trust your nose

    Use the list as a guide, not a promise. Smell is personal.

Strain type tells you how a product is classified; terpenes tell you how it smells. Neither one predicts how it will feel. For the rest of the card, see how to read the Good Life menu; for pre-roll formats, our pre-roll guide; and for every category we carry, the product guide.

Quick answers

Do terpenes change how a product feels?

We stick to aroma. Terpenes are aroma compounds, and how a product feels is personal — everyone's experience is different.

Why don't all products list terpenes?

It depends on what's reported for each product. Many products on our New York menus list them; when there's no Terpenes section, none were listed.

What does mg/g mean?

Milligrams of terpene per gram of product. 10 mg/g is the same as 1%.
